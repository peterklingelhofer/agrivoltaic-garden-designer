# Traditional Ecological Knowledge & Indigenous Agroecology for Agrivoltaic Design

Research brief for a modern agrivoltaic garden designer. Agrivoltaics is structurally an artificial
partial-shade agroforestry system: a photovoltaic canopy imposes a fixed or dynamic overstory on a
cropped understory, exactly the structural problem that dozens of documented traditional and
indigenous polyculture systems have solved with living canopies, water-harvesting geometry, and
temporal succession for centuries to millennia. Each entry below gives a short profile (region,
peoples, structure, mechanism, citation) and a distilled, implementable design rule. Living practices
are described in present tense; historical/archaeological systems in past tense. Knowledge is
attributed to specific peoples where the record documents them; broad "indigenous" language is
avoided except where a source itself spans many unnamed groups (e.g., continent-wide archaeological
complexes).

## 1. AMERICAS

### 1.1 Three Sisters (maize–bean–squash) — Haudenosaunee and Mesoamerican origin, pan-American spread

- **Region/peoples:** Originated in central Mexico ~1000 CE spread; codified agronomically and
  ceremonially by Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) nations of the Northeastern Woodlands, and independently
  elaborated across Mesoamerican, Southwestern, and Southeastern farming peoples.
- **Structure:** Maize planted in mounds; pole beans trellis up the maize stalk; squash/pumpkin
  vines spread along the ground between mounds.
- **Mechanism:** Maize provides vertical structure for climbing beans; beans' root-nodule rhizobia
  fix atmospheric nitrogen benefiting nitrogen-hungry maize; squash's broad leaves form a low
  living-mulch canopy that shades the soil surface, suppresses weeds, and reduces evaporative water
  loss — a two-tier vertical stratification (tall/climbing/ground-cover) analogous to an
  overstory–midstory–groundcover agroforestry stack. Niche complementarity (light, N, water, space)
  rather than mere spatial packing drives the yield advantage.
- **Evidence:** Land Equivalent Ratio (LER) studies show the intercrop out-yields component
  monocultures on a per-area basis even though bean and squash yields are individually suppressed by
  maize competition (root-foraging/niche-complementarity mechanism). Pleasant & Burt (2010)
  reconstructed Haudenosaunee ("Iroquoian") field productivity from historical and experimental data;
  Cryan et al. (2025) directly compared monoculture vs. Three Sisters yield, growth, and labor
  demands.
- **Citations:**
  - Pleasant, J. & Burt, R.F. (2010). "Estimating Productivity of Traditional Iroquoian Cropping
    Systems from Field Experiments and Historical Literature." *Journal of Ethnobiology* 30(1):52–79.
  - Bellow et al. / root-foraging niche complementarity study: "Root foraging elicits niche
    complementarity-dependent yield advantage in the ancient 'three sisters' (maize/bean/squash)
    polyculture," *PLOS ONE* (2015), PMC4416130.
  - Cryan, K. et al. (2025). "Yield, growth, and labor demands of growing maize, beans, and squash in
    monoculture versus the Three Sisters." *Plants, People, Planet*. doi:10.1002/ppp3.10576.
- **Design rule:** *Stack functionally distinct plant guilds by canopy layer under a fixed
  overstory — one structural species, one nitrogen-fixing companion, one shade-tolerant ground-cover
  species — and evaluate combined output with a Land-Equivalent-Ratio-style metric, not per-species
  yield, since individual species yields will legitimately drop while total system output rises.*

### 1.2 Milpa — Mesoamerica (Maya, Nahua, and other Mesoamerican farming peoples)

- **Structure:** A rotational polyculture field, typically maize-bean-squash core plus dozens of
  companion species (chili, tomatillo, amaranth, etc.), embedded in a fallow-forest rotation cycle.
- **Mechanism:** Complementary resource use across light, water, nutrient, and spatial niches; one
  species modifies microclimate favorably for others (shade, humidity, windbreak); high species
  diversity spreads pest/disease/climate risk.
- **Citation:** Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems (2026), "The milpa system as a model of
  agroecological intensification: integrating productivity, ecological functioning, and resilience,"
  *Front. Sustain. Food Syst.* 10:1843955; review synthesis in ResearchGate, "Review of agronomic
  research on the milpa, the traditional polyculture system of Mesoamerica."
- **Design rule:** *Treat the shaded understory as a multi-species guild, not a single companion
  crop — deliberately assign each species a distinct microclimate role (moisture retainer, windbreak,
  pest-diversion) rather than optimizing any one species in isolation.*

### 1.3 Chinampas — Basin of Mexico (Xochimilcans; later administered by the Aztec/Mexica empire)

- **Structure:** Narrow raised planting islands (roughly 6–10 m × 100–200 m) built up in shallow
  lakebeds from canal mud, aquatic vegetation, and willow-leaf litter, separated by canals.
- **Mechanism:** Continuous replenishment of canal mud as fertilizer/topsoil produces long-term
  fertility gain rather than depletion; canal water buffers temperature and supplies sub-irrigation;
  raised-bed geometry improves drainage and aeration. Enabled up to seven harvests per year without
  synthetic inputs.
- **Citation:** Archaeological dating at Xaltocan and Xochimilco (pollen/radiocarbon) documents
  pre-Aztec origins later intensified under Mexica administration; overview in *World History
  Encyclopedia*, "Chinampas: Mexico's Human-Made Agricultural Islands"; academic treatment: Morehart,
  C.T. & Frederick, C. "Chinampa agriculture, surface hydrology, and settlement in the Basin of
  Mexico," *Journal of Field Archaeology* / *Vegetation History and Archaeobotany* literature.
- **Design rule:** *Design the growing substrate as a renewable, actively replenished system (compost
  or mineral-topping cycle) rather than a static bed, and pair beds with adjacent water/thermal-mass
  reservoirs for passive irrigation and microclimate buffering.*

### 1.4 Waru waru / camellones — Lake Titicaca Altiplano (Tiwanaku-era and pre-Inca Andean farmers; still used by contemporary Aymara and Quechua communities)

- **Structure:** Raised soil platforms ("camellones") separated by water-filled canals, built across
  the high-altitude Altiplano.
- **Mechanism:** Canal water acts as a thermal mass, absorbing solar energy by day and releasing it
  at night, buffering the growing surface against the frequent frost events that devastate
  unprotected potato/quinoa crops at altitude; canals also store water against short droughts.
- **Citation:** Kolata, A.L. & Ortloff, C.R. (1989). "Thermal analysis of Tiwanaku raised field
  systems in the Lake Titicaca Basin of Bolivia," *Journal of Archaeological Science* 16(2):233–263;
  recent modeling in *Energies* 18(12):3116 (2025), "Sustainable Thermal Energy Storage Systems: A
  Mathematical Model of the 'Waru-Waru' Agricultural Technique."
- **Design rule:** *Use adjacent thermal-mass reservoirs (water channels, stone, dense soil beds) as
  a passive night-time frost/cold buffer for shade-grown beds, sized and validated the way Kolata &
  Ortloff modeled bed/canal geometry for heat storage capacity.*

### 1.5 Andean vertical archipelago & andenes terracing — Andean polities (Quechua and Aymara-speaking communities; Inca-era intensification)

- **Structure:** Non-contiguous communities maintained direct control of fields at multiple discrete
  elevations ("vertical archipelago"); hillside terraces (andenes) created flat, retained planting
  platforms at each elevation band.
- **Mechanism:** Vertical archipelago spreads production and climate risk across ecological zones
  (self-sufficiency ideal, per Murra); andenes terrace walls absorb daytime heat and release it at
  night, moderating frost risk for roots, while retaining soil and water on steep slopes.
- **Citation:** Murra, J.V. (1972/1975). "El control vertical de un máximo de pisos ecológicos en la
  economía de las sociedades andinas" (from the 1969 Lewis Henry Morgan Lectures, "The Archipelago
  Model of Vertical Control in the Andes"); overview in *Vertical archipelago*, IntechOpen (2018),
  "Horizontal and Vertical Archipelagoes of Agriculture and Rural Development in the Andean Realm."
- **Design rule:** *Where a design spans multiple microclimate zones (e.g., varying shade density
  under a PV array), deliberately allocate distinct cultivar/species sets per zone rather than one
  variety across the whole array — replicate the risk-spreading logic of the vertical archipelago at
  the scale of a single garden's shade gradient.*

### 1.6 Amazonian terra preta and agroforestry home gardens — pre-Columbian Amazonian peoples (multiple unnamed cultures across the Basin, active ca. 500 BCE–950 CE)

- **Structure:** Anthropogenic dark, carbon-rich soils ("Amazonian Dark Earths") formed in and around
  middens, home gardens, and outlying agroforestry fields; polyculture agroforestry intensified
  alongside dark-earth formation from ~2,000 years BP.
- **Mechanism:** Low-heat, smoldering biomass burning produced black carbon retained in soil (up to
  70× background levels), creating persistent fertility (soils remain fertile 1,000–2,000 years after
  active management ceased) — the archaeological precedent for modern biochar.
- **Citation:** Glaser, B., Lehmann, J. & Zech, W. (2002). "Ameliorating physical and chemical
  properties of highly weathered soils in the tropics with charcoal – a review," *Biology and
  Fertility of Soils* 35:219–230; dark-earth landscape synthesis: "Dark earths and the human built
  landscape in Amazonia," *Journal of Archaeological Science* (2013); Eos.org feature, "The
  Nutrient-Rich Legacy in the Amazon's Dark Earths."
- **Design rule:** *Build soil fertility with recalcitrant carbon amendments (biochar co-composted
  with nutrients) under the shaded, cooler microclimate a PV canopy creates — the practice that
  originated as a byproduct of Amazonian home-garden management, not a synthetic-fertilizer
  substitute.*

### 1.7 Eastern Agricultural Complex — independent domestication center, eastern North America (multiple ancestral communities, Riverton culture and later Adena/Hopewell-associated groups)

- **Structure:** Independent domestication (from ~7000 cal BP, intensified by ~3800–3500 BP) of
  goosefoot (*Chenopodium berlandieri*), marsh elder/sumpweed (*Iva annua*), sunflower (*Helianthus
  annuus*), and squash, initially cultivated on floodplains and later uplands.
- **Mechanism:** One of the world's few independent centers of plant domestication; selection for
  larger seed size, reduced dormancy, and non-shattering seed heads for ease of harvest — largely
  displaced by maize agriculture but instructive for underused/"lost crop" landrace diversity.
- **Citation:** *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 380(1926):20240192 (2025), "The
  sleeping crops of eastern North America: a new synthesis."
- **Design rule:** *Do not default to globally dominant crops for shade-adapted beds; evaluate
  regionally domesticated/underused landrace crops that may already carry shade or floodplain-origin
  tolerance traits.*

### 1.8 Pacific Northwest Coast forest gardens — Ts'msyen and Coast Salish peoples (coastal British Columbia)

- **Structure:** Deliberately tended understories at village sites — crabapple, hazelnut, cranberry,
  wild plum, wild cherry in the shrub layer, wild ginger and wild rice root in the herb layer — set
  within/beneath the coastal conifer canopy.
- **Mechanism:** Selective removal of non-useful trees and retention/planting of useful fruit-, nut-,
  and medicine-bearing species produced anthropogenic multistorey forest patches; these forest gardens
  measurably out-perform surrounding unmanaged conifer forest in species and functional diversity more
  than 150 years after active cultivation ceased.
- **Citation:** Armstrong, C.G. et al. (2021). "Historical Indigenous land-use explains plant
  functional trait diversity," *Ecology and Society* 26(2):6 (SFU News release, April 2021); follow-up
  historical-ecology study: Armstrong, C.G. et al. (2022). "Historical ecology of forest garden
  management in Laxyuubm Ts'msyen and beyond," *Ecosystems and People* 18(1). doi:10.1080/26395916.2022.2160823.
- **Design rule:** *Under a permanent partial-shade structure, favor a multistorey shrub/understory
  guild of regionally native, useful perennials over annual monocrop rows — the Armstrong et al.
  finding is that this produces self-sustaining biodiversity gains that persist for a century-plus
  with minimal ongoing management, a strong argument for perennial guild design under fixed PV
  shade.*

### 1.9 Zuni waffle gardens and Hopi dry farming — Zuni (A:shiwi) and Hopi peoples, U.S. Southwest

- **Structure:** Zuni waffle gardens: small (~0.6 m square) sunken planting cells bordered by raised
  soil berms, topped with a thin sand/gravel "mineral mulch." Hopi dry farming: deep seed planting
  (to reach subsurface moisture) and akchin (runoff-fed) fields sited at arroyo mouths below the
  mesas to capture storm runoff and eroded sediment.
- **Mechanism:** Waffle-cell berms trap and localize scarce irrigation water per plant; sand mulch
  breaks capillary action, cutting evaporative water loss (mulch can roughly double basin water-use
  efficiency per garden practitioner accounts); akchin placement harvests both water and
  nutrient-bearing sediment from a much larger effective watershed than the planted area itself.
- **Citation:** Ford, R.I. treatment of Southwestern dry farming; site-specific technical synthesis in
  University of Arizona Press, *Settlement, Subsistence, and Society in Late Zuni Prehistory*, ch. 7,
  "Zuni Area Environment and Agricultural Technology."
- **Design rule:** *Apply water-harvesting geometry at two scales simultaneously: micro (sunken
  basin + inert mulch layer per plant, cutting evaporation) and macro (site planting zones to
  intercept runoff from a catchment larger than the bed itself) — directly transferable to gutter/
  runoff design under a PV array's drip-line.*

## 2. AFRICA

### 2.1 Zaï pits and demi-lunes — Sahelian farmers (documented especially among Mossi farmers of Burkina Faso, e.g. Yacouba Sawadogo's revival of zaï)

- **Structure:** Small dry-season-dug pits (20–40 cm diameter, 10–20 cm deep) or half-moon-shaped
  bunds (2–3 m diameter, 15–30 cm deep, opening uphill), each amended with organic matter (compost/
  manure).
- **Mechanism:** Pits/bunds concentrate scarce rainfall and wind-blown organic debris precisely at
  the planting point; termite activity attracted by the buried organic matter further improves soil
  structure and infiltration. Documented yield gains of 60–90% for millet/sorghum vs. flat
  cultivation on degraded land.
- **Citation:** TAAT-Africa technology catalog, "Zaï Pits: Water Harvesting and Soil Improvement";
  World Bank open-knowledge synthesis on Sahelian regreening; academic case study series in *VertigO*
  (DOAJ).
- **Design rule:** *Micro-catch and amend at the point of planting rather than broadcasting water/
  compost across a bed — every plant position under a PV array should be its own small water- and
  nutrient-harvesting basin, sized to the reduced/redirected rainfall pattern the panels create.*

### 2.2 Faidherbia albida parkland agroforestry — Sahelian and Sudanian-zone farming communities (widespread across West and East African parklands)

- **Structure:** Scattered, widely spaced *F. albida* trees retained/planted within crop fields
  (maize, sorghum, millet) in a parkland pattern.
- **Mechanism:** *F. albida* exhibits **reverse phenology** — it is leafless during the rainy/crop
  growing season (minimizing shade and root competition for the understory crop) and comes into full
  leaf during the dry season (providing dry-season fodder shade and depositing nitrogen-rich leaf
  litter before the next planting). This is a naturally evolved dynamic-shading precedent directly
  analogous to seasonally-adjustable or single-axis-tracking PV canopies.
- **Citation:** Roupsard, O. et al. (1999). "Reverse phenology and dry-season water uptake by
  *Faidherbia albida* (Del.) A. Chev. in an agroforestry parkland of Sudanese west Africa," *Functional
  Ecology* 13(4):460–472.
- **Design rule:** *Treat shade as a scheduled, seasonally-variable resource rather than a constant —
  if the PV mounting allows tracking/tilt, minimize shading during the understory crop's peak growth
  window and maximize it during heat-stress or dormancy periods, mirroring F. albida's inverted
  leaf-out calendar.*

### 2.3 Chagga home gardens — Chagga people, Mt. Kilimanjaro, northern Tanzania

- **Structure:** A ~2,000-year-old multistorey home-garden system: overstory shade/timber trees,
  banana as a mid-canopy layer, coffee as a shrub layer, understory food and medicinal herbs — over
  100 useful plant species across ~40 families documented, embedded within a landscape supporting
  ~520 total vascular plant species.
- **Mechanism:** Vertical stratification mimics native montane forest structure, providing the
  banana/coffee layers with a buffered, humid, partially shaded microclimate while diversifying yield
  across food, fodder, fuel, and cash-crop functions.
- **Citation:** Fernandes, E.C.M., O'Kting'ati, A. & Maghembe, J. (1985). "The Chagga homegardens: a
  multistoried agroforestry cropping system on Mt. Kilimanjaro, Northern Tanzania," *Agroforestry
  Systems* 2:73–86; Hemp, A. (2006). "The banana forests of Kilimanjaro: biodiversity and conservation
  of the Chagga homegardens," *Biodiversity and Conservation* 15:1193–1217.
- **Design rule:** *Model the target microclimate not as "artificial shade over a single crop" but as
  a designed forest-analog stack (canopy → mid-tier fruiting layer → shrub cash crop → herb
  understory), and select the mid/shrub layer species specifically for their known shade tolerance
  under real forest canopy, as coffee and banana are under Chagga management.*

### 2.4 Ethiopian enset systems — southern Ethiopian highland peoples (e.g., Sidama, Wolaita, Kambata, Gurage, and others)

- **Structure:** Home gardens combining the perennial staple enset (*Ensete ventricosum*, "false
  banana") with coffee, annual crops, livestock, and multipurpose trees on small plots; farmers
  actively maintain dozens of named enset landraces per community.
- **Mechanism:** Enset's dense, tall pseudostems create a stable perennial shade/windbreak structure
  around which shorter, more climate-sensitive crops and coffee are nested; landrace diversity spreads
  drought and disease risk and is actively curated through household propagation choices.
- **Citation:** Tsegaye, B. & Struik, P.C. and colleagues, "Indigenous knowledge, use and on-farm
  management of enset (*Ensete ventricosum*) diversity in Wolaita, Southern Ethiopia," PMC4025559;
  "Exploring the multiple contributions of enset for sustainable management of home garden agroforestry
  system in Ethiopia," *Trees, Forests and People* (2022).
- **Design rule:** *Encode landrace/cultivar diversity as a first-class design variable, not an
  afterthought — allow multiple named varieties of the same species to be planted in one shaded bed,
  since on-farm diversity itself (not just species diversity) is the documented risk-spreading
  mechanism.*

### 2.5 Push-pull technology — smallholder maize farmers of western Kenya and East Africa (developed by icipe researchers, notably Zeyaur Khan, in direct collaboration with local farmers)

- **Structure:** Maize intercropped with *Desmodium* spp. (a shade-tolerant, nitrogen-fixing legume
  ground cover) between rows, bordered by a perimeter of Napier grass (*Pennisetum purpureum*).
- **Mechanism:** Desmodium emits volatiles that repel stemborer moths ("push") while also
  suppressing/eliminating parasitic *Striga* weed via shading and root allelopathy; Napier grass border
  emits volatiles that attract the moths away from the maize ("pull"). A single companion planting
  scheme delivers pest control, weed control, soil nitrogen, and livestock fodder simultaneously.
- **Citation:** Khan, Z.R. et al. (2008). "On-farm evaluation of the 'push-pull' technology for the
  control of stemborers and striga weed on maize in western Kenya," *Field Crops Research*
  106(3):224–233; Khan, Z.R. et al. (2008). "Economic performance of the 'push-pull' technology for
  stemborer and Striga control in smallholder farming systems in western Kenya," *Crop Protection*
  27(7):1084–1097.
- **Design rule:** *Design companion-planting guilds around chemical-ecology function (repellent /
  attractant / allelopathic ground cover), not only physical shade/structure — a shaded understory
  under PV panels is an opportunity to place low-light-tolerant push-pull-style companions that manage
  pests without pesticide input.*

## 3. ASIA / OCEANIA

### 3.1 Japanese solar sharing (soraru shearingu) — direct lineage to agrivoltaics, rooted in satoyama land-use ethic

- **Structure:** Elevated, narrow (~24-cell) PV modules mounted well above crop height with wide
  inter-panel gaps, allowing continued cultivation beneath.
- **Mechanism:** Devised specifically to let crops use only the light fraction they can utilize while
  routing the remainder to electricity generation; narrow module width and gap spacing were engineered
  to minimize shading unevenness and prevent splash erosion beneath drip lines.
- **Citation:** Nagashima, A. (2015, Japanese; 2020, English). Foundational "solar sharing" text; Chiba
  prefecture first-farm case documented in "Evolution of agrivoltaic farms in Japan," *AIP Conference
  Proceedings* 2361(1):030002 (2021); institutionalized by Japan's Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry
  and Fisheries directives (March 2013, May 2018); by 2019, 1,992 agrivoltaic farms (560 ha) existed
  across 46 of 47 prefectures.
- **Design rule:** *This is agrivoltaics' own direct ancestor: adopt Nagashima's core design
  constraint directly — narrow module width with light-permeable gaps sized to the specific crop's
  shade tolerance, rather than a solid canopy, and treat crop light-use efficiency data (not just
  panel output) as a first-order sizing input.*

### 3.2 Javanese pekarangan home gardens — Javanese communities, Indonesia

- **Structure:** Home gardens surrounding residences with 3–4 vertical canopy strata: tall coconut/
  jackfruit canopy, fruit-tree mid-layer, shrub/perennial-vegetable layer, herb/annual/root-crop
  ground layer, often combined with small livestock.
- **Mechanism:** High species packing across strata maximizes light capture at every canopy height and
  diversifies household income/nutrition; for intensively managed households, pekarangan can supply
  roughly a fifth of household income plus a meaningful share of daily vitamin C from a small yard.
- **Citation:** Michigan State University pekarangan study summarized in "PEKARANGAN JAVA-INDONESIA
  PAPER" (CSUS, MSU); Christanty, L. et al., "Traditional agroforestry in West Java: the Pekarangan
  (homegarden) and Kebun-Talun (annual-perennial rotation) cropping systems."
- **Design rule:** *Default to 3–4 explicit vertical strata (not 1–2) in any shaded polyculture bed
  design, and budget for a mixed subsistence/cash-crop portfolio across strata rather than a single
  target crop.*

### 3.3 Kerala home gardens — Kerala, India (including documented tribal homegarden traditions, e.g. in the Attappady valley)

- **Structure:** Dense multistorey home gardens with documented species counts from under 5 to over
  40 crop/tree species per homestead; canopy strata from tall timber/coconut down to spice vines and
  tuber ground layer.
- **Mechanism:** High structural and species diversity is quantitatively linked (via Shannon-Wiener
  and Margalef diversity indices in field inventories) to system resilience and multi-output
  productivity per unit area.
- **Citation:** Kumar, B.M. & Nair, P.K.R. (2004). "The enigma of tropical homegardens," *Agroforestry
  Systems* 61:135–152; Peyre, A. et al. (2006). "Dynamics of homegarden structure and function in
  Kerala, India," *Agroforestry Systems* 66:101–115; Jacob, V.J. & Alles, W.S. (1987) foundational
  Kerala homegarden structural surveys.
- **Design rule:** *Use a formal diversity index (Shannon-Wiener or equivalent) as a design target
  metric for a shaded polyculture bed, not just crop count — it lets a designer compare candidate
  planting plans quantitatively the way agroforestry researchers compare real home gardens.*

### 3.4 Rice–fish–duck integrated systems — originated in China, reintroduced/adapted from Japan across East and Southeast Asia (China, Japan, South Korea, Vietnam, Philippines); intensively documented among Hani communities of Yunnan

- **Structure:** Rice paddy stocked with fish and/or ducks; ducks graze weeds and pests between rice
  rows, fish forage on pests/algae, both contribute manure directly to the paddy.
- **Mechanism:** A single flooded field produces three yield streams (grain, fish, poultry) while the
  animals perform mechanical weeding and biological pest control, cutting pesticide costs by roughly
  half in documented Hani-terrace case studies, and substantially raising total provisioning-service
  value versus rice monoculture.
- **Citation:** "Valuation of Ecosystem Services for the Sustainable Development of Hani Terraces: A
  Rice–Fish–Duck Integrated Farming Model," *International Journal of Environmental Research and
  Public Health* 19(14):8549 (2022).
- **Design rule:** *Look for a second and third functional yield stream from the same shaded planting
  area (e.g., pollinator forage, small livestock forage, or aquaculture in a wet agrivoltaic
  configuration) rather than optimizing a single crop's yield in isolation.*

### 3.5 Mulberry-dyke and fish-pond system — Pearl River Delta and Taihu Lake region farming communities, southern/eastern China (GIAHS-recognized at Huzhou)

- **Structure:** Ponds excavated from low-lying land, banked with dykes planted in mulberry (feeding
  silkworms) or other crops; a closed nutrient loop: pond mud fertilizes mulberry -> mulberry leaves
  feed silkworms -> silkworm frass feeds fish -> fish waste fertilizes pond mud.
- **Mechanism:** A fully circular, zero-external-input nutrient loop linking terrestrial (mulberry/
  silk) and aquatic (fish) production, developed over roughly 600 years and formally recognized as a
  Globally Important Agricultural Heritage System by FAO in 2017.
- **Citation:** Ruddle, K. & Zhong, G. (1988). "Integrated Agriculture-Aquaculture in South China: The
  Dike-Pond System of the Zhujiang Delta," Cambridge University Press; FAO GIAHS designation
  documentation, "Zhejiang Huzhou Mulberry-dyke and Fish-pond System."
- **Design rule:** *Design explicit material loops between the shaded planting zone and any adjacent
  system (compost, aquaculture, livestock) so that a byproduct of one component (biomass, manure,
  runoff nutrients) is the deliberate input of another, rather than each subsystem being provisioned
  independently.*

### 3.6 Ifugao rice terraces (Philippine Cordilleras) and Hani rice terraces (Yunnan, China)

- **Structure:** Ifugao: five integrated components — communal forest (muyong), swidden farms, rice
  terraces, settlement, and water bodies/irrigation, built into steep mountain slopes 800–1,500 m.
  Hani: analogous forest–village–terrace–river vertical zonation across ~70,000 ha in Yunnan,
  developed and maintained by Hani communities for over 1,300 years.
- **Mechanism:** Forest retained above the terraces (muyong in Ifugao) creates a micro-watershed that
  filters and supplies water year-round to the paddies below by gravity; the vertical
  forest-village-terrace-water stacking is itself a designed watershed, not just a planting pattern.
- **Citation:** FAO GIAHS, "Ifugao Philippines" and "Hani Rice Terraces, China" designation profiles;
  UNESCO World Heritage inscription documentation, "Rice Terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras"
  (1995).
- **Design rule:** *Treat water supply as a designed upstream system, not an input to be assumed —
  where feasible, pair a shaded/planted zone with a dedicated upstream catchment or filtration element
  engineered specifically to feed it by gravity, as muyong forests feed Ifugao terraces.*

### 3.7 Balinese subak — Balinese farming communities, Bali, Indonesia (developed by the 9th century CE)

- **Structure:** A cascading network of farmer-managed irrigation cooperatives ("subak"), each
  associated with a water temple; a hierarchy of water temples from a single upstream source down
  through the watershed coordinates planting, harvesting, and fallow scheduling across the whole
  watershed.
- **Mechanism:** Temple-mediated scheduling synchronizes fallow/flood periods across many farms
  simultaneously, which functions as area-wide pest/pathogen management (breaking pest life cycles)
  while equitably allocating scarce irrigation water — a socio-technical governance solution as much as
  a physical one. Lansing's ethnographic/computational modeling work demonstrated this coordination
  function empirically.
- **Citation:** Lansing, J.S. (1991). *Priests and Programmers: Technologies of Power in the Engineered
  Landscape of Bali*, Princeton University Press; UNESCO World Heritage inscription (2012), "Cultural
  Landscape of Bali Province: the Subak System as a Manifestation of the Tri Hita Karana Philosophy."
- **Design rule:** *For any multi-plot/multi-user deployment (e.g., a shared community agrivoltaic
  garden), build in explicit shared scheduling/coordination logic (staggered planting, synchronized
  fallow) as a software feature, not just per-plot optimization — the subak lesson is that coordinated
  timing across plots is itself a pest-management and water-equity mechanism.*

### 3.8 Hawaiian ahupuaʻa and loʻi kalo — Native Hawaiian (Kānaka Maoli) land and water management

- **Structure:** Ahupuaʻa are wedge-shaped land divisions running from mountain to sea, each
  containing the full range of elevation zones needed for self-sufficiency; within them, loʻi kalo are
  irrigated taro pondfields fed via engineered ditches (ʻauwai) diverting stream water.
- **Mechanism:** Continuous-flow irrigated pondfields dramatically outproduce dryland taro cultivation
  (roughly 5–15× reported yield advantage), while the ahupuaʻa's full mountain-to-sea span guarantees
  each community access to every ecological zone's resources without a single monoculture staple risk.
- **Citation:** Handy, E.S.C. & Handy, E.G. (1972). *Native Planters in Old Hawaii: Their Life, Lore,
  and Environment*, Bishop Museum Press; Mueller-Dombois, D. treatment, "The Hawaiian Ahupuaʻa Land Use
  System: Its Biological Resource Zones and the Challenge for Silvicultural Restoration," Bishop
  Museum.
- **Design rule:** *Model watershed-scale resource zoning (elevation/moisture gradient -> distinct
  planting zone) as a first-class concept even in a single-site design — a garden with a shade
  gradient under a PV array is a miniature ahupuaʻa, and each zone should get species suited to its
  specific water/light regime rather than one blanket planting plan.*

### 3.9 Australian Aboriginal fire-stick farming and murnong (yam daisy) management — Aboriginal peoples of temperate southeastern Australia, notably documented on the Victorian Western Plains

- **Structure:** Regular, patchy, low-intensity ("cool") burning regimes applied by Aboriginal women
  and communities to grasslands, both to reveal murnong (*Microseris scapigera*, yam daisy) tubers for
  harvest and to maintain the open grassland habitat the plant requires.
- **Mechanism:** Fire clears grass cover for visibility/access, recycles nutrients, and maintains
  murnong's preferred disturbance regime; sustained harvesting and burning may have extended murnong's
  range and density in ways debated by researchers as functionally close to low-intensity cultivation.
  This remains an actively contested area of scholarship (the "Dark Emu" debate) and should be
  presented with appropriate epistemic caution rather than as settled fact.
- **Citation:** Gott, B. (1983). "Ecology of root use by the Aborigines of southern Australia," *Archaeology
  in Oceania* 18(1):59–72; Bowman, D.M.J.S. et al. (2008). "The 'fire stick farming' hypothesis:
  Australian Aboriginal foraging strategies, biodiversity, and anthropogenic fire mosaics," *PNAS*
  105(supplement 2); Denham, T. (2023). "Putting the Dark Emu debate into context," *Archaeology in
  Oceania* — a corrective/contextualizing review of overstated cultivation claims.
- **Design rule:** *Use disturbance (mowing/pruning/managed dieback analogous to controlled fire) as a
  deliberate design tool to maintain an open, harvestable growth stage in a perennial shade bed, rather
  than only planting/watering — and when documenting this practice in-product, flag the ongoing
  scholarly debate explicitly instead of stating contested claims as settled.*

## 4. EUROPE / MEDITERRANEAN

### 4.1 Iberian dehesa (Spain) / montado (Portugal) — agrosilvopastoral system of Extremadura, Andalusia, and Alentejo, developed and maintained by generations of Iberian smallholders and estate managers

- **Structure:** Widely spaced holm oak (*Quercus ilex*) and cork oak (*Q. suber*) over grazed
  pasture/cereal understory; an explicit, quantified shade-agriculture analog.
- **Mechanism:** Field studies document that transmitted radiation, soil moisture, and
  crop/pasture growth vary systematically with distance from the tree canopy, so the dehesa is a
  partial-shade gradient system with microclimate benefit (moderated temperature extremes, reduced
  evapotranspiration near trunks) traded against reduced light farther from the canopy edge.
  Montero, Moreno & Bertomeu (2008) back the SHAPE of the light-versus-distance curve; their
  regression coefficients are paywalled and were never obtained, so no distance-resolved magnitude
  in this project rests on a measured figure. The soil-moisture literature reports categorical
  beneath-canopy and beyond-canopy zones rather than a distance function at all.
- **Citation:** Montero, G., Moreno, G. & Bertomeu, M. (2008), *Agroforestry Systems*
  73:233-244 (`montero2008-dehesa-light`), for the light-versus-distance shape. An earlier draft of
  this document cited "Marcos, G.M. et al." here; that work does not exist and was never a source,
  see `docs/VERIFICATION.md` section 3. Moreno, G. & Pulido, F.J. (2009). "The Functioning, Management and Persistence of
  Dehesas," in *Agroforestry in Europe*, Springer; Portuguese hydrological modeling: Fabião, A. et al.,
  "Modeling Soil Water Dynamics and Pasture Growth in the Montado Ecosystem Using MOHID Land," *Water*
  10(4):489 (2018).
- **Design rule:** *This is the closest documented real-world analog to agrivoltaics available: use
  dehesa distance-from-trunk radiation/soil-moisture curves as an empirical starting template for
  distance-from-panel-edge light and moisture modeling under a PV array, rather than assuming uniform
  shade across a bed.*

### 4.2 Mediterranean multistorey olive/vine/cereal intercropping — Mediterranean Basin farming communities from Roman antiquity to present (Italy, Greece, Iberia)

- **Structure:** Olive trees intercropped with cereals (barley, wheat) and/or legumes in the alley
  space, historically also combined with vine and fig in a documented Roman-era three-tier system.
- **Mechanism:** Moderate canopy shade from widely spaced olive trees has been shown to *increase*
  intercropped barley biomass and grain/protein yield in controlled studies, likely via improved soil
  nitrogen availability and moderated microclimate, while olive vegetative growth also benefits from
  the intercrop.
- **Citation:** Mantzanas, K. et al. or similar, "Comparative assessment of agroforestry performances
  between pea and barley intercropping with olive tree under Mediterranean conditions," *Agricultural
  Systems* 98:619 (2024); "Intercrop of olive trees with cereals and legumes in Chalkidiki, Northern
  Greece" (ResearchGate); historical synthesis, "The green granary of the Empire? Insights into olive
  agroforestry in Sicily (Italy) from the Roman past and the present," *Journal of Archaeological
  Science: Reports* / regional journal (2024).
- **Design rule:** *Do not assume shade strictly trades off against understory yield — under moderate,
  well-spaced canopy, some cereal/legume yield and quality metrics documented under olive canopy
  actually improve; a design tool should model shade as a potentially yield-positive variable for
  specific crop-shade combinations, not only a yield tax.*

### 4.3 Coppice and pollarding — traditional woodland management across lowland temperate Europe (widely documented in medieval and early-modern England, France, Germany, and Central Europe)

- **Structure:** Periodic cutting of trees to a stump (coppicing) or to a height above browse line
  (pollarding), stimulating vigorous regrowth from the same rootstock/trunk on a repeatable multi-year
  rotation.
- **Mechanism:** Provides a renewable, repeat-harvest structural/fuel/fodder resource without
  replanting, while maintaining a dynamic, cyclically-changing canopy density and light regime at
  ground level that itself created and sustained high understory biodiversity in managed woodlands.
- **Citation:** Nicolescu, V.-N. et al. (2018). "Silvicultural Guidelines for European Coppice
  Forests," EuroCoppice COST Action; Sciencedirect (2021), "Dwindling coppice woods in Central Europe –
  Disappearing natural and cultural heritage," *Forest Ecology and Management*.
- **Design rule:** *Treat the overstory/shade structure itself as manageable on a rotation, not fixed
  for the system's lifetime — where physically feasible (adjustable panel tilt/height, or literal
  companion tree pruning within an agrivoltaic array), schedule periodic canopy-density changes on a
  multi-year cycle to reset light regime and understory composition, as coppice rotation does.*

## 5. CROSS-CUTTING PRINCIPLES — encodable design rules

These recur across nearly every system above and are the priority candidates for encoding as explicit
design rules or constraints in agrivoltaic-garden design software:

1. **Vertical stratification / multistorey canopy layering.** (Chagga, pekarangan, Kerala, Three
   Sisters, Pacific Northwest forest gardens, Amazonian home gardens.) Design rule: require every
   planting plan to declare at least 2–4 explicit canopy tiers with species assigned per tier, keyed
   to each tier's expected light level under the PV structure.
2. **Nurse plants and shade nurseries.** (Faidherbia albida parkland, milpa companion shading, dehesa
   oak-pasture gradient.) Design rule: allow a "nurse species" role distinct from a "target crop" role
   in the data model, with nurse species selected for the microclimate service they provide (shade,
   wind, humidity, N-fixation) rather than for direct yield.
3. **Wind and thermal buffering.** (Waru waru thermal mass, andenes stone-wall heat storage, dehesa
   canopy microclimate, Push-pull perimeter planting.) Design rule: model adjacent thermal-mass or
   windbreak elements (water, stone, dense hedge) as first-class microclimate modifiers of a bed's
   effective growing conditions, not just the panel's own shade.
4. **Water-harvesting geometry.** (Zaï pits/demi-lunes, Zuni waffle gardens, akchin fields, chinampas,
   subak, Ifugao muyong.) Design rule: support both micro-scale (per-plant basin/mulch) and
   macro-scale (catchment/runoff routing sized larger than the planted area) water-harvesting geometry
   as configurable design elements, explicitly tied to a PV array's drip-line and runoff shadow.
5. **Temporal succession and relay cropping.** (Coppice rotation, F. albida reverse phenology, subak
   synchronized fallow, Andean vertical archipelago risk-spreading across elevation/season.) Design
   rule: make planting schedules time-aware and shade-schedule-aware — pair each crop's growth-stage
   calendar against the panel's own seasonal/diurnal shade pattern (and tilt schedule, if tracking),
   rather than treating shade as a static site attribute.
6. **Seed sovereignty and landrace adaptation.** (Enset landrace curation, Eastern Agricultural
   Complex underused domesticates, milpa companion diversity.) Design rule: support multiple named
   landraces/cultivars of a single species as distinct plantable entities with their own trait data
   (not just "tomato" but named, shade-tolerance-annotated varieties), and prioritize regionally
   adapted landraces over globally standardized commercial cultivars where data exists.
7. **Polyculture risk-spreading.** (Three Sisters LER advantage, milpa diversity, Kerala/pekarangan
   species counts, Andean vertical archipelago.) Design rule: default to portfolio-style yield
   reporting (aggregate output/LER across a multi-species bed) rather than single-crop yield
   maximization as the primary optimization target.

## 6. ETHICS AND ATTRIBUTION

### 6.1 The governing frameworks

- **CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance** (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control,
  Responsibility, Ethics) — developed by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance / International
  Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group (Research Data Alliance). CARE is explicitly designed to
  complement, not replace, the open-data FAIR principles: Indigenous Peoples' data — including
  traditional ecological/agricultural knowledge, oral histories, and cultural-site information —
  requires governance by the originating community, not just open publication.
  - Citation: Carroll, S.R. et al. (2020). "The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance," *Data
    Science Journal* 19:43; Carroll, S.R. et al. (2021). "Operationalizing the CARE and FAIR Principles
    for Indigenous data futures," *Scientific Data* 8:108.
- **Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit-Sharing** (in force since 2015, under the UN Convention on
  Biological Diversity) — a binding international legal framework requiring prior informed consent and
  mutually agreed benefit-sharing terms before genetic resources *and associated traditional knowledge*
  are accessed and utilized commercially or in research, in countries that are party to it. This is a
  legal, not merely ethical, obligation where applicable.
- **Local Contexts TK Labels and Notices** — a practical, community-controlled metadata system (TK
  Labels for cultural-heritage/knowledge attribution and access conditions; BC Labels for biocultural/
  genetic-resource-linked data) that communities themselves apply to assert attribution, access, and
  use conditions on specific pieces of knowledge or data, distinct from copyright.
  - Citation: localcontexts.org, "TK Labels" and "BC Labels" program documentation.

### 6.2 The core distinction: published academic literature vs. community-held knowledge

Everything cited in sections 1–5 above is **published, peer-reviewed academic literature** — already
in the public scholarly record, authored (in most cases) by non-community academic researchers
describing systems, sometimes in collaboration with or crediting named community experts (e.g., Khan
et al.'s push-pull work was co-developed with western Kenyan farmers; Nagashima's solar-sharing
concept is a named individual's modern invention inspired by, not identical to, indigenous practice).
Citing this literature with full academic attribution (author, year, journal) is standard scholarly
practice and does not by itself raise CARE/Nagoya concerns.

The concerns arise specifically when a product moves **beyond citing the literature** toward:
encoding specific plant-variety knowledge, seed genetics, ritual/ceremonial timing rules, place-specific
site knowledge, or community protocols as reusable, redistributable, or commercializable software
logic (e.g., "optimal planting date per traditional ceremonial calendar," a named community's specific
unpublished companion-planting ratios, or seed/germplasm data tied to a landrace a community actively
stewards).

### 6.3 Concrete product guidance

**Do:**
- Attribute every design rule in this document (and any derived from it) to the *specific* people(s)
  or the specific published source, never to "indigenous farmers" or "traditional cultures" generically.
  Where a source itself is a broad archaeological complex spanning unnamed groups (e.g., Eastern
  Agricultural Complex), say so explicitly rather than inventing specificity.
- Cite the academic literature in-product (e.g., an "About this design rule" panel linking to the
  journal citation) exactly as done above.
- Distinguish clearly, in the UI/documentation, between (a) a design principle derived from published
  research about a historical or archaeological system with no living rights-holder to consult (e.g.,
  Tiwanaku waru waru, chinampas) and (b) a design principle derived from a living community's ongoing
  practice (e.g., Chagga home gardens, Sahelian zaï-pit farmers, Ts'msyen/Coast Salish forest gardens).
  For (b), note that the cited academic description is a researcher's account, not the community's own
  voice, and avoid presenting it as if the software has the community's endorsement.
- If the product ever seeks to include material beyond what's in published academic literature —
  direct collaboration with a knowledge-holding community, specific germplasm, unpublished practice
  detail — treat that as requiring the community's free, prior, and informed consent and a benefit-
  sharing agreement under Nagoya-Protocol-style terms, and consider applying Local Contexts TK Labels
  to any resulting data/content so the community retains asserted control over its reuse.
- Credit named individual innovators distinctly from communities (e.g., Akira Nagashima for solar
  sharing, Yacouba Sawadogo for the modern zaï revival, Zeyaur Khan/icipe for push-pull) — conflating a
  named scientist's invention with "ancient indigenous wisdom" is its own form of misattribution.

**Do NOT:**
- Do not encode or commercialize specific unpublished seed/landrace genetic data, ceremonial or
  ritual agricultural calendars, or site-specific sacred/restricted knowledge, even if a determined
  search could surface fragments of it online — publication in a blog or tourism article is not the
  same as a community's consent to commercial reuse.
- Do not present composite or synthesized "indigenous wisdom" design defaults (a single generic
  "traditional companion planting" preset) without naming the specific system and people(s) it is
  drawn from; pan-indigenous generalization erases the real diversity and specific authorship
  documented above (a Zuni waffle-garden water-harvesting rule and a Chagga vertical-stratification
  rule are not interchangeable and should not be merged into one unattributed "ancient wisdom"
  feature).
- Do not claim community endorsement, certification, or partnership unless it has actually been
  sought and granted; a citation to academic literature about a community's practice is not a
  partnership with that community.
- Do not monetize (e.g., as a premium feature or paid dataset) any content that traces to
  community-held rather than published knowledge without a benefit-sharing arrangement in place.
